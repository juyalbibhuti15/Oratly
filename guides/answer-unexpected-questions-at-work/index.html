How to answer an unexpected question from your manager

Your manager turns to you: “Are we still on track?” You know the project, but choosing a clear first sentence feels harder with everyone listening.

By Bibhuti Juyal · Oratly

The short answer

Take a brief pause. Give your answer first, add the reason your manager needs, and finish with a useful next step. If you do not know, state what you can confirm, what you need to check, and when you will follow up. Practice this structure with different questions so you can use your own words.

Give your answer somewhere to go

A practical starting structure is answer, reason, next step. You do not need to fill every part if the question only needs a short factual reply.

  1. Answer: Say the point your manager asked for. For a status question, that might be “We are two days behind.”
  2. Reason: Add the fact that explains the answer. Choose the detail that changes the decision, rather than the whole project history.
  3. Next step: State what you recommend, what happens next, or what decision you need.

For a yes-or-no question, start with your position and any essential qualification. For an open question, start with your main point. A direct answer can still include uncertainty.

Worked example: “Are we still on track?”

The following fictional workplace example and coaching notes were written for this guide.

The situation

Your team is preparing a software release. A vendor file arrived late. Import is complete, and validation is still in progress. Your manager asks about the Friday launch.

A response that buries the answer

“So, we had the vendor file issue, and there were a few messages about it yesterday. We got the file in eventually, and the team has been working through it. There are still checks to do, so I think we need to see where we are.”

The explanation contains useful facts, but your manager still has to work out whether the launch date is reliable and what you recommend.

A clearer response
“Friday’s launch is at risk. The vendor file arrived two days late; the import is complete, but validation is still open. I recommend waiting for Thursday’s checks before confirming the release date.”
  • Directness: The opening answers the status question.
  • Structure: The response moves from the current position to the reason and then the recommendation.
  • Conciseness: The details explain the risk without replaying every message.

Use your own project’s verified facts and decision process when you practice a similar answer.

When you do not know the answer

Separate the information you have from the information you still need. A confident delivery should not turn a guess into a fact.

“I can confirm that the import is complete. I haven’t checked the validation results yet. I’ll review them with the test lead and send you an update by 2 p.m.”

Choose a follow-up time you can meet. If the question itself is unclear, clarify the decision first: “Are you asking whether testing is complete, or whether we should keep the Friday date?”

Handle the follow-up without restarting the story

Your manager may ask a narrower question after your first answer. Respond to that question instead of repeating the whole update.

Manager: “What do you need from me?”

You: “Approval to keep the test lead focused on validation through Thursday. That gives us the information needed to confirm the launch date.”

Finish once you have answered. You can offer more detail with a simple question: “Would you like the validation detail?”

Try it out loud

A short practice exercise

  1. Choose a real question: Use a recent work moment, such as “What is the biggest risk?” Replace names and confidential details with fictional ones.
  2. Record one response: Aim for roughly 30–60 seconds as an exercise constraint. Start with the answer, then add the reason and next step where useful.
  3. Review one thing: Listen for whether your opening answers the question. If it does, check whether the next sentences follow a clear order.
  4. Try again: Improve one part of the response. Then change the question so you practice organizing a fresh answer.

Use your phone camera or Oratly. The exercise is useful when it gives you a specific change to make on the next attempt.

Common questions

What can I say while I gather my thoughts?

Try “Let me think for a moment.” Take a brief pause, choose your main point, and begin. If you need information you do not have, explain what you will check and when you will follow up.

Should I memorize answers before a meeting?

Prepare the facts and likely questions. Practice an answer structure and use your own words. Try variations of the question so your rehearsal includes responding to something unexpected.

How short should my answer be?

Give enough information to answer the question and support the next decision. A quick factual question may need one sentence; a recommendation may need context. Use 30–60 seconds as a practice constraint, then adjust to the real conversation.
